What Machiavelli Can Teach You About Office Politics (and What to Ignore)

Published 27 Oct 2026 · By RemoteKazi Team · 4 min read

Ruthless" Machiavelli quotes are going viral on Facebook and TikTok, but many of them were never written by him. We went back to what he actually wrote in The Prince and pulled out six lessons that help you handle office politics, plus the advice that will damage your career if you follow it.

Posts promising "ruthless lessons in power" from Niccolò Machiavelli are all over social media right now, often with dramatic quotes and the warning that "not everyone should know this". They get thousands of likes and shares. But a lot of what is shared is not Machiavelli at all.

Machiavelli was a diplomat and civil servant in Florence, Italy, about 500 years ago. After losing his government job, he wrote The Prince (published in 1532, after his death), a short guide on how rulers gain and keep power. It is blunt, sometimes shocking, and still widely read. Read carefully, it has useful lessons for anyone trying to get ahead at work. Read through viral memes, it can push you into mistakes that cost you your job.

First, check the quote

Two of the most shared "Machiavelli quotes" are not his:

  • "The end justifies the means." He never wrote this sentence. It is a later summary of his ideas, and a misleading one.
  • "In the forest there is no good or evil, only the strong and the weak..." We could not find this line in The Prince or his other major works. It appears to be a modern saying that has been attached to his name online.

The lesson for your career is the same one we give about job adverts: before you share it or act on it, check where it came from.

Six lessons that work in the office

1. Be respected, but never hated. Machiavelli's famous conclusion is that it is "much safer to be feared than loved" if a leader cannot be both. What is often left out is the next part: he insists a leader must avoid being hated, above all by never taking what belongs to others. At work, the version that helps you is firm and fair. Set clear standards, follow through on them, and never take credit, money or opportunities that belong to colleagues. People forgive strictness; they do not forgive being cheated.

2. Judge people by what they do, not what they promise. Machiavelli warns that support bought with favours disappears when times get hard. In an office, notice who delivers on deadlines and who keeps their word, not who is friendliest at tea break.

3. Choose who you listen to carefully. He warns rulers that their courts are full of flatterers, and advises picking a few wise people who will tell the truth when asked. In your career, find two or three honest mentors who will give you real feedback on your work, your CV and your interview answers, not just praise.

4. You are judged by the company you keep. Machiavelli wrote that the first impression of a ruler's intelligence comes from the people around him. At work, the people you spend time with, and the gossip you join in, shape how managers see you.

5. Change always has opponents. He observed that anyone who introduces a new way of doing things makes enemies of everyone who benefited from the old way, and gets only lukewarm support from those who might gain. If you are pushing a new system or idea at work, expect resistance, build support before you announce it, and show early results.

6. Prepare when things are calm. Machiavelli compared luck to a flooding river: you cannot stop the flood, but you can build defences in good weather. In career terms, that means building savings, skills and contacts while you are employed, not after a layoff. Keep your CV updated and your clearance documents current, so you can move quickly when an opportunity appears.

The advice to ignore

Some "Machiavellian" tips shared online are a fast way to lose your job or your reputation:

Viral adviceWhy it backfires at work
"Deceive people to get ahead"Lies are eventually discovered, and dishonesty is grounds for dismissal and can follow you through references
"Keep people afraid of you"Fear without fairness becomes hatred, the one thing Machiavelli warned against; toxic managers lose their best people
"Trust no one"Careers in Kenya run on referrals and networks; people recommend those they trust
"Win at any cost"Breaking rules or the law can end a career; Chapter Six checks for government jobs exist for this reason

Machiavelli was writing for 16th-century princes fighting wars, not employees with contracts, HR policies and the Employment Act. Take the realism; leave the ruthlessness.

Putting it to work

Office politics are real in every workplace, from banks in Upper Hill to county offices and start-ups. The people who do well are usually not the most ruthless. They are reliable, fair, well prepared and careful about who they trust. If your current workplace rewards the opposite, it may be time to move.
