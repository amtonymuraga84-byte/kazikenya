Money Sent to You by Mistake? A Dandora Case Shows Why You Must Return It

Published 26 Oct 2026 · Updated 26 Oct 2026 · By RemoteKazi Team · 3 min read

A Dandora man is awaiting sentencing after admitting he spent Ksh150,000 of a Ksh250,000 payment that reached his bank account by mistake. The case is a clear warning: keeping money that is not yours is a crime in Kenya, and returning it is simple if you act quickly.

Mistaken transfers happen every day in Kenya. One wrong digit on an M-Pesa number or a bank account is enough to send a salary, a school-fees payment or a supplier's money to a stranger. A case decided in Nairobi this week shows what can happen when the person who receives that money decides to keep it.

What happened in the Dandora case

According to the Directorate of Criminal Investigations (DCI), a complainant accidentally sent Ksh250,000 to the wrong bank account on September 4, 2026. The account holder did not report it. Instead, he withdrew Ksh150,000 in three separate transactions before his bank froze the remaining balance.

Detectives based at Wilson Airport obtained a court order to inspect the account, confirmed the withdrawals and later traced the man to Dandora, where he was arrested on October 23. When he appeared before the Senior Principal Magistrate's Court at JKIA on October 26, he faced five charges, including intentionally withholding an electronic payment sent to him in error. He pleaded guilty to all of them and was remanded in custody. Sentencing is set for November 17, 2026.

What the law says

The DCI cited the Computer Misuse and Cybercrimes Act, 2018, which makes it an offence to knowingly keep an electronic payment that was meant for someone else. According to the DCI, a conviction can bring a fine of up to Ksh200,000, a prison term of up to two years, or both.

The key word is intentionally. Receiving money by mistake is not a crime. The offence begins when you realise the money is not yours and choose to spend it, move it or hide it. Banks and mobile money providers keep full records of every transaction, so a wrong payment can almost always be traced back to the person who received it.

If someone sends money to you by mistake

  • Don't spend or move it. Leave the money where it is, even if you need it.
  • Don't send it back yourself to a number that calls you. A common scam is to send a fake "wrong payment" message and then ask you to refund it. Check your actual balance first.
  • Report it to your provider. For M-Pesa, call Safaricom customer care or follow the reversal steps in the transaction message. For a bank transfer, call or visit your bank and ask them to handle the reversal.
  • Keep a record of the date, amount and who you spoke to.

If you sent money to the wrong person

  • Act fast. For M-Pesa, request a reversal immediately through Safaricom; reversals are much easier before the money is withdrawn.
  • For bank transfers, call your bank's customer care right away and follow up in writing with the transaction reference.
  • If the recipient refuses to return it, report the case at the nearest police station or to the DCI with your transaction records.

Why this matters for job seekers and workers

Many people now receive salaries, casual-work payments and freelance income straight into M-Pesa or a bank account. If you work in a role that handles payments, such as cashier, teller, accounts assistant or agent, knowing these rules protects both you and your employer. A criminal record for withholding money can end a career in finance before it starts, and many employers now ask for a police clearance certificate before hiring.

The lesson from Dandora is simple: a payment you were not expecting is not a windfall. Report it, return it, and move on.
