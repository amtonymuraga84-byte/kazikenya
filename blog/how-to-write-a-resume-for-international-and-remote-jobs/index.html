How to Write a Resume for International and Remote Jobs (Not a Kenyan CV)

Published 13 Nov 2026 · By RemoteKazi Team · 5 min read

A CV that works for a Kenyan employer can quietly fail with a foreign or remote one. Harvard's careers office has published clear guidance on what international recruiters expect. Here is what it says, what to change on your CV, and a before-and-after example you can copy.

More Kenyans are applying to remote companies and to jobs abroad. Many of them send the same CV they would send to a county government or a local bank, with an ID number, date of birth, a passport photo and three referees, and they hear nothing back.

The problem is usually not their experience. It is the format. Employers in the US, and many remote-first companies, expect a short resume, and some of what Kenyan CVs traditionally include counts against you there.

Mignone Center for Career Success (MCS), Harvard College's careers office, keeps a public guide on writing a strong resume. Its advice is written for American students, but it is a useful picture of what a foreign recruiter is looking for. Below is a summary of its main points, adapted for Kenyan job seekers.

Resume or CV: what's the difference?

In Kenya, "CV" is the everyday word for the document you attach to any application. In the US, a "CV" usually means a long academic record, and almost every other job asks for a resume: a tight summary of your strongest skills and results.

Typical Kenyan CVInternational resume
Length2–3 pages is commonAs short as possible; one page is typical early in your career
PhotoOften includedLeave it out
Date of birth, ID, marital statusOften includedLeave them out
RefereesListed at the endNot listed; give them when asked
StyleDuties, sometimes in paragraphsShort bullet points showing results

The Harvard guide lists a photo, your age or gender, and a list of references among the things to leave off. It also warns that conventions differ by country, so always check what the employer in that country expects.

The five mistakes that get resumes rejected

The guide names five common faults. Each one is easy to fix before you click "Apply":

  1. Spelling and grammar errors. Read it aloud, then ask someone else to read it.
  2. Missing or wrong contact details. Check that your email and phone number (with +254) are correct.
  3. Passive language. "Was responsible for" tells a recruiter nothing. Start with what you did.
  4. Poor layout. If a recruiter can't scan it in seconds, they move on.
  5. No results. Duties describe the job; results describe you.

Write bullet points that start with an action

Harvard's guide groups strong verbs by the kind of skill they show. Pick verbs that fit what you actually did:

  • Leading: led, coordinated, supervised
  • Communicating: presented, negotiated, drafted
  • Analysing: researched, analysed, audited
  • Technical work: designed, built, automated
  • Training others: trained, coached, mentored
  • Organising: implemented, streamlined, scheduled
  • Helping people: supported, advised, resolved

Then add what changed: a number, a time saved or a result. Use the formula verb + task + result.

Before (Kenyan CV style):

  • Responsible for handling customer complaints at the Nakuru branch.

After (resume style):

  • Resolved 35+ customer complaints a day at the Nakuru branch, cutting repeat complaints by a third in six months.

Only use numbers you can explain in an interview. A recruiter in another country may still call your former manager.

Style rules to follow

The guide says resume language should be specific, active and factual, written "to express, not impress". In practice:

  • No "I" or "we". Write "Managed a team of four", not "I managed a team of four."
  • No long paragraphs. Short bullet points only.
  • No slang or unexplained abbreviations. A US recruiter may not know KCSE, TSC or KRA. Write "Kenya Certificate of Secondary Education (KCSE)" once, or describe the grade instead.
  • Most recent first. List jobs and education in reverse order, newest at the top.
  • Lead with the job title, not the date. Put dates on the right.
  • Explain gaps. A missing year raises questions; one honest line answers them.
  • Check the PDF. Formatting can shift when you convert from Word, so open the PDF before you send it.

Tailor it to every job

The guide says your resume won't get you the job on its own, but it does decide whether you get an interview. Not every line has to match the role, but the resume should show the skills that employer values. Read the advert, note its key words, and make sure the ones that truly describe you appear in your bullet points.

Using AI without sounding like everyone else

Harvard's advice on AI tools is sensible: use them to edit, not to write. AI can help you tighten a bullet point, find a stronger verb, or check that you've covered the advert's key words. But if it writes the whole resume, the result tends to sound generic, and recruiters can tell.

A simple approach: write your own rough bullet points first, then ask an AI tool to "make this bullet point shorter and start with an action verb, without adding anything that isn't true". Check every change.

Quick checklist

  • One page if you're early in your career; nothing older than about ten years unless it's relevant
  • No photo, date of birth, ID number, marital status or referee list
  • Every bullet starts with an action verb, and most show a result
  • Kenyan terms explained once
  • Contact details checked, file saved as a PDF named with your name
