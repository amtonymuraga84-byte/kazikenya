7 Early Warning Signs Your Body Is Sending, and When to See a Doctor

Published 30 Oct 2026 · By RemoteKazi Team · 5 min read

Most serious illnesses give signals long before a diagnosis. A symptom that fades in a few days is rarely a worry, but one that lingers for weeks, keeps coming back or arrives with new symptoms deserves a doctor's attention. Here are seven signs to watch, and what working Kenyans can do about them.

Long hours, tight deadlines and a daily commute make it easy to brush off how you feel. Tiredness gets blamed on work, a cough on the dust, weight changes on a new diet. Those explanations are often right. But many people diagnosed with a serious condition later recall months of vague symptoms they ignored, and catching a problem early usually means simpler, cheaper and more effective treatment.

Seven signs worth acting on

1. Tiredness that rest doesn't fix

Feeling drained after a hard week is normal. Fatigue that continues for weeks despite good sleep is not. Anaemia, an underactive thyroid, diabetes, sleep apnoea, low vitamin B12 and depression can all cause it, and a basic blood test can rule most of them out.

2. Unexplained weight changes

Losing more than 4 to 5 kilograms over six months without trying calls for a check-up, especially alongside a persistent cough, night sweats or poor appetite. Possible causes include an overactive thyroid, diabetes and, less often, cancer. Unexpected weight gain together with fatigue, dry skin or hair loss may point to an underactive thyroid.

3. Frequent headaches or dizziness

Most headaches are tension-related or migraines. See a doctor if your headaches change pattern, wake you up, get worse over weeks, or come with vision changes, nausea or numbness. Regular dizziness, particularly with hearing changes, may need blood pressure or inner-ear checks.

4. Changes in appetite, sleep or digestion

When poor appetite, broken sleep and ongoing constipation or diarrhoea appear together, they often share one cause, such as a thyroid problem or depression. Heartburn that doesn't respond to treatment, a lasting change in bowel habits or new difficulty swallowing should be reported promptly.

5. Shortness of breath or chest discomfort

Getting breathless on stairs you used to climb easily is not just "getting older." It can signal a heart or lung problem. In women, heart trouble often shows up as breathlessness or fatigue rather than classic chest pain. A cough lasting more than three weeks should be tested, since tuberculosis remains a real risk in Kenya.

6. Frequent infections or slow-healing wounds

Repeated infections, or small cuts that take too long to heal, may mean your immune system or circulation isn't working well. Poorly controlled diabetes is the most common cause. Women who suddenly start getting repeated urinary infections should also be checked for diabetes.

7. Persistent joint pain or body aches

Morning joint stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes, or swelling in the small joints of both hands and feet, can indicate rheumatoid arthritis. Bone pain that wakes you at night, or back pain that gets worse at rest, especially with fever or weight loss, needs proper evaluation.

Symptoms that need care straight away

Don't wait two weeks for these. Go to a hospital or call for help if you have:

  • Chest pain or pressure
  • A sudden, severe headache
  • Sudden weakness or numbness in an arm, leg or one side of the face
  • Sudden vision loss or difficulty speaking
  • Blood in your urine or stool, or coughing up blood

When to book a check-up

If any of the seven signs lasts more than two to three weeks without a clear reason, book a consultation. You don't need a dramatic symptom to justify a visit.

Even if you feel fine, adults from about age 30 should consider a yearly check for conditions that often develop silently:

Blood sugarPrediabetes caught early can often be reversed
Blood pressureHigh blood pressure damages the heart and kidneys for years before symptoms
CholesterolRaises heart attack and stroke risk without warning signs

If close relatives have had diabetes, heart disease, high blood pressure or certain cancers, ask your doctor whether you should start screening earlier. A general practitioner can handle most routine adult screening and refer you to a specialist when needed.

Making time for your health as a worker

Busy schedules are the main reason working people skip check-ups. A few practical steps help:

  • Check your medical cover. Many employers offer medical insurance that includes an annual wellness check or outpatient visits. Ask HR what your scheme covers, and confirm what your SHA cover includes at your nearest facility.
  • Know your sick leave rights. Under Kenya's Employment Act, workers who have served at least two months are entitled to sick leave with pay, usually backed by a medical certificate. Keep copies of any doctor's notes.
  • Book early or on weekends. Many clinics and labs open early or on Saturdays, so tests don't eat into a working day.
  • Watch work stress. Stress can cause real physical symptoms, from headaches to stomach trouble and chest tightness. Read our guide on how work stress affects your relationships, and if your job is the main cause, it may be time to look at other openings.

Quick answers

Can stress cause physical symptoms? Yes: fatigue, headaches, digestive problems, poor sleep and chest tightness. A doctor can help tell stress-related symptoms apart from a medical condition.

Is unexplained weight loss always serious? Not always, but it should be checked. Common causes such as thyroid problems and diabetes respond well to early treatment.

How often should adults get a check-up? About once a year from age 30 for core tests. Others, like mammograms or colonoscopies, follow intervals based on your age, results and risk.
