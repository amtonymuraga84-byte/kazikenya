How to Negotiate Your Salary in Kenya

By RemoteKazi Team · Updated 3 Nov 2026

Many people in Kenya accept the first offer they receive because negotiating feels risky or rude. In reality, most employers expect some discussion, and a calm, well-prepared conversation can raise your pay or improve your package without damaging the relationship. This guide shows you how to research your worth, handle the salary question, and negotiate an offer.

Understand gross pay and net pay

Before you discuss numbers, be clear about what they mean. In Kenya, salaries are usually quoted as gross monthly pay: the amount before deductions. Your take-home, or net pay, is lower after statutory deductions, which include:

  • PAYE income tax, deducted by your employer according to KRA tax bands
  • NSSF pension contributions
  • SHIF contributions for health cover under the Social Health Authority
  • The Affordable Housing Levy

Depending on your pay, these deductions can take a large share of your gross salary, so always confirm whether a figure is gross or net. Use an up-to-date Kenyan net pay calculator to see what an offer means for your take-home pay, because the rates change from time to time.

Step 1: Research the going rate

Your strongest argument in any negotiation is evidence. Before the interview, find out what similar roles pay:

  • Job adverts that state a salary range for similar roles in your county
  • Salary surveys and guides, such as our nurse salary guide
  • People in the field: former classmates, colleagues and professional groups
  • Government scales: public service jobs are paid by job group, and the scales are published

Pay varies a lot by sector and location. The same role usually pays differently at an international NGO, a bank, a private SME and a county government, and Nairobi often pays more than smaller towns.

Step 2: Know your numbers

Before any conversation, decide three figures:

  • Your walk-away point: the lowest gross salary you could accept, based on your real monthly costs.
  • Your target: the figure you would be happy with, based on your research.
  • Your opening range: a range whose bottom is close to your target, leaving room to negotiate.

Step 3: Handle "What are your salary expectations?"

This question often comes up in the first call or interview. Some tips:

  • Give a range, not a single number, based on your research. For example: "Based on similar roles in Nairobi, I'm looking for between KSh 80,000 and 95,000 gross a month, depending on the full package."
  • Say it confidently, without apologising or explaining your personal expenses.
  • Avoid revealing your current salary unless it helps you. If asked, you can say: "I'd rather focus on the value of this role. Based on my research, the market range is..."
  • For fixed-scale jobs, such as government roles, there is usually little room to negotiate the basic pay. You can simply confirm you are comfortable with the advertised scale.

Step 4: When the offer arrives

Do not accept or reject on the spot. Thank the employer, show that you are pleased, and ask for the offer in writing with time to review it, such as one or two working days. That is a normal, professional request.

Then look at the whole package, not just the basic salary:

  • Basic pay, and allowances such as house, transport, airtime or risk allowance
  • Medical cover for you and your family, and its limits
  • Pension contributions beyond NSSF
  • Annual leave days and any bonus or commission
  • Contract type and length, probation period and notice terms
  • Training, study support and chances for promotion
  • Working hours and remote work options

Step 5: Make your counter-offer

If the offer is below your target, respond politely with a specific request and a reason based on your research or your skills.

"Thank you for the offer. I'm excited about the role and the team. Based on my research for similar positions and the experience I'd bring in managing supplier reconciliations, I was hoping for a gross salary closer to KSh 90,000. Is there flexibility to reach that figure?"
  • Ask for one specific number, slightly above what you would accept.
  • Base it on value, not personal need. "My rent has gone up" is not a reason for the employer to pay more. "I bring three years of directly relevant experience" is.
  • Stay friendly and brief, then let them respond.

If they can't raise the salary

Many employers have fixed salary bands. If the basic pay cannot move, you can negotiate other parts of the package:

  • A higher allowance, such as transport or house allowance
  • A salary review after probation, written into your offer letter
  • Better medical cover, or cover for your dependants
  • Extra leave days, flexible hours or some remote working days
  • Paid training, exam fees or study leave
  • A sign-on payment or an earlier start on the bonus scheme

Know when to accept and when to walk away

If the final offer meets your walk-away point and the role is good for your career, accept it graciously. Experience, skills and a good reference can be worth more than a small difference in pay. If the offer is below what you can live on, it is reasonable to decline politely. You never know when the employer might come back with a better role.

Get it in writing

Never resign from your current job based on a verbal offer. Ask for a written offer letter or contract that shows the agreed salary, allowances, benefits, start date and any promises made during negotiation, such as a review after probation. Read it carefully, and if something differs from what you agreed, raise it politely before you sign.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Accepting immediately without seeing the full package
  • Giving a figure without researching the market first
  • Confusing gross and net pay
  • Making demands or threats, or negotiating by bluffing about other offers that do not exist
  • Negotiating again and again after you have accepted
