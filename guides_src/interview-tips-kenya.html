Getting an interview means your CV has done its job. Now the employer wants to know three things: can you do the work, will you fit in, and do you really want the role? Good preparation matters far more than natural confidence. This guide covers how to prepare, ten common questions with example answers, and what to bring on the day.

Know which kind of interview you are facing

  • Phone screening: a short call, often from HR, to confirm your experience and salary expectations. Treat it as a real interview and take it somewhere quiet.
  • Panel interview: common in government, county, university and NGO jobs. Three to seven people take turns asking questions and score your answers against a marking sheet.
  • Online interview: on Zoom, Microsoft Teams or Google Meet. These are common for remote roles and NGOs.
  • Aptitude or technical test: many banks, graduate programmes and large companies test numerical, verbal or job-specific skills before or after the interview.
  • Practical test: drivers, chefs, technicians and teachers may be asked to demonstrate the work.

If the invitation does not say what to expect, it is fine to reply and ask politely about the format and how long it will take.

Before the day

  1. Study the advert again. List each requirement and write one example from your experience that proves you meet it. Panels often score you directly against these requirements.
  2. Research the employer. Know what they do, their main products or programmes, where they operate and any recent news. Their website, annual report and LinkedIn page are good places to start.
  3. Prepare your stories. Have five or six real examples ready: a problem you solved, a time you worked under pressure, a conflict you handled, a mistake you learned from, and a result you are proud of.
  4. Practise out loud. Answer the questions below to a friend, or record yourself on your phone. Aim for answers of about one to two minutes.
  5. Plan the logistics. Know the exact location, plan for Nairobi traffic, and aim to arrive 15 minutes early. For online interviews, test your link, camera, microphone and internet the day before, and have mobile data as a backup.

Answer with the STAR method

For questions that start with "Tell me about a time when...", structure your answer in four parts:

  • Situation: where you were and what was happening
  • Task: what you were responsible for
  • Action: what you did. Say "I", not "we".
  • Result: what happened, ideally with a number
"At my attachment with a county health office (situation), I was asked to help clear a backlog of 2,000 unfiled patient records before an audit (task). I set up a simple filing system by ward and date, and trained two colleagues to use it (action). We finished a week early, and the audit raised no record-keeping issues (result)."

10 common questions and how to answer them

1. Tell me about yourself.

This is not a request for your life story. Give a 60-second summary covering your current role or studies, your most relevant experience, one achievement and why you are interested in this job. Leave out your home village, family and hobbies unless they relate to the role.

2. Why do you want this job?

Connect what the employer does with what you want to do. "I need a job" is honest but weak. Mention something specific about the organisation, and how the role builds on your experience.

3. Why should we hire you?

Pick the two or three requirements that matter most in the advert, and give brief proof that you meet each one. End with what you would bring in the first few months.

4. What is your greatest strength?

Choose a strength that matters for this job and back it with an example. "I am organised" means little on its own. "I am organised: I managed stock for three shops and cut shortages by half" is convincing.

5. What is a weakness you are working on?

Choose a real weakness that is not central to the job, and explain what you are doing about it. Avoid disguised boasts such as "I work too hard".

"I used to find public speaking difficult. I joined my church's youth committee so I could present updates every month, and I'm now comfortable leading meetings."

6. Describe a problem you solved.

Use the STAR method. Pick a problem related to the work you would do in this job, and focus on your own actions and the result.

7. Tell me about a time you worked in a team.

Show that you can cooperate, share credit and handle disagreement. Explain your specific role in the team and what the team achieved.

8. How do you handle pressure and deadlines?

Describe your method, such as making a list, prioritising and communicating early if something will be late, then give a real example of a tight deadline you met.

9. Where do you see yourself in five years?

The panel wants to know whether you will stay and grow. Talk about building expertise and taking on more responsibility in this field, ideally within the organisation. Avoid saying you plan to leave for further studies abroad or to start your own business, even if you might.

10. What are your salary expectations?

Research pay for similar roles before the interview, using job adverts, salary guides and people in the field. Give a realistic range of gross monthly pay, for example "between KSh 60,000 and 75,000 depending on the full package". For government jobs the pay is usually fixed by job group, so you can say you are comfortable with the advertised scale. Our salary negotiation guide has more detail.

Government and county interviews

Public service interviews follow a stricter process. Expect a panel with a scoring sheet, and questions about the Constitution, values and principles of public service, and the specific department. You will usually be asked to bring original documents for verification. These can include:

  • Your national ID and original academic and professional certificates
  • A certificate of good conduct from the DCI
  • Clearance from KRA, HELB, the EACC and a credit reference bureau, for senior roles in particular

These clearances can take days or weeks, so start early. Our blog post on Chapter Six clearance documents explains how to get each one.

What to bring and what to wear

  • Two printed copies of your CV, plus original certificates and copies if requested
  • Your national ID
  • A pen and notebook with your prepared examples and questions
  • Clean, smart clothes suited to the employer. A suit or smart official wear is safest for banks, offices and government. Neat smart-casual is fine for most start-ups and field roles.

Online interview tips

  • Sit facing a window or light, with a plain background
  • Look at the camera, not the screen, when you speak
  • Put your phone on silent and tell people at home you are busy
  • Join five minutes early, with the employer's phone number ready in case the connection fails

Questions to ask the panel

Almost every interview ends with "Do you have any questions for us?" Always have one or two ready. It shows real interest.

  • What would success in this role look like after six months?
  • What are the biggest challenges facing the team right now?
  • How is performance reviewed, and what training is available?
  • What are the next steps in the process, and when can I expect to hear back?

After the interview

Send a short thank-you email within a day, repeating your interest in the role. If you have not heard back by the date they gave you, a polite follow-up is fine. If you do not get the job, you can ask for brief feedback. Many employers will share it, and it will make your next interview stronger.

A final warning: a real employer will never ask you to pay for an interview, a medical test, training or a uniform before you are hired. If that happens, it is almost certainly a scam. Read how to spot a fake job advert.
