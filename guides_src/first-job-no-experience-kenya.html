"Experience required" is the most frustrating line in a job advert when you are trying to get your first job. Every year, hundreds of thousands of young Kenyans finish college or university and compete for a limited number of entry-level jobs. The good news is that employers hiring graduates are not really looking for years of experience. They are looking for evidence that you can learn, show up and do the work. This guide shows you how to build that evidence and land your first job.

Understand what employers want from a beginner

When an employer hires someone with no formal work experience, they are taking a chance. You reduce that risk by showing:

  • Reliability: you finish what you start and turn up on time.
  • Basic workplace skills: clear communication, Excel and email, and working in a team.
  • Interest in the field: you have taken steps to learn about it on your own.
  • Ability to learn quickly: you have picked up new skills before.

Everything below is about creating proof of these four things.

1. Count the experience you already have

Many graduates have more experience than they realise. Include on your CV:

  • Industrial attachment and internships: describe what you did and achieved, not just where you were.
  • Casual and part-time work: working in a shop, as an M-Pesa agent, in a family business or as a tutor shows responsibility and customer skills.
  • Volunteering: church, community, school or NGO work all count.
  • Leadership: class representative, club official or student union roles.
  • Projects: your final-year project, research, a small business, or something you built or organised.

Our CV guide shows how to describe these as results instead of duties.

2. Target entry-level roles

Focus your applications where beginners are actually hired:

  • Graduate trainee and management trainee programmes at banks, manufacturers and large companies
  • Internships, including government and NGO internship programmes, many of which lead to permanent jobs
  • Junior and assistant roles: office assistant, junior accountant, customer service agent, sales representative, data clerk
  • Call centres and customer support, which often train new hires from scratch
  • Sales roles, which reward effort and results more than qualifications

Browse current entry-level jobs in Kenya, and do not be put off by adverts asking for one or two years of experience if you meet most of the other requirements. Attachments, internships and part-time work can count towards that.

3. Build skills employers pay for

Short, practical skills can make you more employable than another certificate in theory. Many can be learned free or cheaply online:

  • Microsoft Excel: useful for almost every office job. Learn formulas, sorting, filtering and pivot tables.
  • Digital marketing and social media management
  • Data entry, data analysis and basic bookkeeping, including tools like QuickBooks
  • Customer service and sales skills
  • Computer packages and typing speed
  • A driving licence, which opens doors to field, sales and logistics jobs

Add each completed course to your CV, and practise the skill on a real task so you can talk about it in an interview.

4. Network, even if you don't think you have a network

Many jobs in Kenya are filled through referrals before they are advertised. Networking simply means letting people know what you are looking for:

  • Tell your former lecturers, attachment supervisors and classmates what kind of work you want.
  • Create a complete LinkedIn profile, connect with people in your field, and follow companies you want to work for.
  • Attend career fairs, industry events and free workshops in your town.
  • Join professional associations in your field. Many have student or graduate membership.
  • Ask people already in the job for a short conversation about how they got started. Most people are happy to help.

5. Apply smarter, not just more

Sending the same CV to hundreds of jobs rarely works. Instead:

  • Apply to fewer jobs, but tailor your CV and cover letter to each one. Our cover letter guide includes advice for people with no experience.
  • Keep a simple list of where you applied, when, and what happened.
  • Apply early. Many employers shortlist as applications arrive.
  • Follow up politely if you have not heard back after the date they gave.

6. Prepare well for interviews

As a graduate, your interview is your biggest chance to stand out. Prepare examples from your studies, attachment and other activities, and practise answering common questions out loud. Our interview guide covers 10 common questions with example answers.

7. Consider stepping-stone options

Your first job does not have to be your dream job. A role that builds experience, skills and references can get you to the next one faster. Other options worth considering:

  • Volunteering with an NGO or community organisation in your field
  • Freelance or remote work, such as writing, data annotation or virtual assistance
  • Short contracts and temporary roles, which can turn into permanent positions
  • Starting small on your own, such as tutoring or a small business, which shows initiative and builds real skills

Protect yourself from scams

Young job seekers are a favourite target for job scams. A genuine employer will never ask you to pay for an interview, training, a uniform or a "slot". Read our guide on how to spot a fake job advert.

Look after yourself

A long job search can be discouraging, and rejection is part of the process for almost everyone. Set small weekly goals, such as five tailored applications and one new skill, keep a routine, and talk to friends and family about how you are feeling. Each application and interview makes the next one better.
