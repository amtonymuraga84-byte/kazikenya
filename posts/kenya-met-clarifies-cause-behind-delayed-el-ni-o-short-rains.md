Many parts of Kenya expected the October–December short rains to arrive weeks ago, especially after months of talk about a wet El Niño season. Instead, several regions have stayed dry. The Kenya Meteorological Department has now explained why.

## What the weather office said

Hannah Kimani, Director of Public Weather Services at the Kenya Meteorological Department, said low-pressure systems forming over the northern Indian Ocean have temporarily stopped a key rain belt from moving south over Kenya. Those systems pulled moisture away from the country, which is why many areas stayed dry at a time when they would normally be getting rain.

## The science in plain words

Kenya's short rains are driven by a band of clouds and rain that circles the globe near the equator. Scientists call it the Intertropical Convergence Zone, or ITCZ. Every year it moves north and then back south with the seasons. When it passes over Kenya on its way south, between October and December, it brings the short rains.

In a normal year:

- Western Kenya usually starts receiving these rains in the first week of October.
- Coastal areas can see the season begin as early as September.

This year, storms over the northern Indian Ocean acted like a roadblock. They held the rain belt further north for longer than usual, so the rains that would normally reach Kenya were delayed. The weather office says it is monitoring the situation and will update its forecasts as conditions change.

## What a late season means for farmers

Planting too early, before the rains are established, is one of the most common and costly mistakes in a delayed season. Seed can dry out or fail to germinate, and farmers may have to buy and plant again. Useful steps include:

- **Follow your county forecast.** The weather office publishes county-level outlooks; local agricultural extension officers can explain what they mean for your crops.
- **Wait for steady rain** before planting rather than reacting to a single shower.
- **Consider early-maturing varieties** if the season starts late, so crops can mature before the rains end.
- **Plan water use** for livestock and kitchen gardens until the rains arrive.

## What it means for jobs and prices

Agriculture employs more Kenyans than any other sector, much of it seasonal and casual. When planting is delayed, the work of preparing land, planting and weeding shifts later too, which can leave casual farm workers without income for several weeks. A short or late season can also push up the price of maize, beans and vegetables in markets a few months later.
