In a statement released on Monday, October 26, 2026, the DCI said the case dates back to September 4, 2026, when a complainant accidentally sent Ksh250,000 to the suspect’s bank account.

Rather than reporting the error and returning the funds, the man allegedly treated the money as his own. Investigators found that he withdrew Ksh150,000 in three separate transactions before his bank stepped in and blocked the rest of the balance.

The DCI described it as a windfall that was never his to keep, noting that his decision to spend the money has now landed him in court.

Detectives based at Wilson Airport took up the case after the mistaken transfer was reported. They obtained a court order at the Jomo Kenyatta International Airport (JKIA) Law Courts, allowing them to examine the suspect’s bank account.

The account records confirmed the three withdrawals. Officers then launched a search for the suspect, who was eventually traced to a hideout near Total Petrol Station in Dandora, Nairobi, and arrested on October 23, 2026.

On Monday, the suspect appeared before the Senior Principal Magistrate’s Court at JKIA, where he faced five charges. One of them was intentionally withholding an electronic payment sent to him in error. The DCI did not disclose the details of the other four counts.

He pleaded guilty to all of them, was convicted and remanded in custody. Sentencing is set for November 17, 2026.

**What the Law Says About Money Sent by Mistake**

The DCI pointed to Section 25 of the Computer Misuse and Cybercrimes Act, 2018, which makes it an offence to deliberately keep an electronic payment that was meant for someone else. Anyone convicted under this section faces:

A fine of up to Ksh200,000
A prison term of up to two years
Or both

Importantly, the law targets intentional withholding. Simply receiving money by mistake is not a crime in itself. What matters is how the recipient responds once they realise the funds are not theirs.

**A Lesson for Mobile and Bank Users**

As digital payments become part of daily life in Kenya, mistaken transfers are increasingly common. This case serves as a reminder that money sent in error should be reported to the bank or mobile service provider and returned, rather than spent. What may look like a lucky break can quickly turn into a criminal record.
