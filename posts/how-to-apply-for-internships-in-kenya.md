Many Kenyan graduates finish university with a good degree and no work experience, and then find that most job adverts ask for two or three years of it. An **internship** is the usual way out of that trap. It gives you a supervisor who can vouch for you, real tasks to put on your CV, and often a foot in the door for a permanent job.

This guide explains how internships work in Kenya, who can apply, and how to prepare an application that is not rejected on a technicality.

## Two kinds of internship

**Government internships.** The Public Service Commission (PSC) advertises internship programmes in cycles, called cohorts, for ministries, state departments and agencies. Each advert says which qualifications are wanted, how many places there are, and when applications close. Applications are made online, and adverts can be for degree holders, diploma holders or specific professions, so read each advert for the group it targets.

**Private sector and NGO internships.** Banks, telecoms, manufacturers, hospitals, law firms, NGOs and start-ups run their own programmes. These are advertised on company career pages, LinkedIn and job boards, and some are only given to students or recent graduates. Many are paid, some are not, and the length varies, so always ask in writing about stipend and duration before you accept.

## What Parliament's bill says about interns

Kenya has been working on a law to set basic rules for public sector internships. According to the Parliament of Kenya's bill digest, the Public Service Internship Bill, 2022 would:

- limit an internship to a maximum of **twelve months**, unless a professional body requires a longer period before registration;
- cover unemployed graduates with a certificate, diploma or degree from a recognised institution, as well as people who must intern to register with a professional body;
- require institutions to advertise internships on their website and in at least two newspapers with national circulation;
- give interns a monthly stipend, set by regulations or by the institution, plus personal accident insurance and leave.

The digest records that the National Assembly passed the bill on 3 December 2025 and that it then went to the Senate. A bill is not law until both Houses pass it, the President signs it and it is published, so check Parliament's website for its current status before you rely on any of these rules.

## Documents to prepare before you apply

Having your documents ready is half the work, because adverts often close within two to three weeks. Prepare clear PDF scans of:

- Your **national ID** (or passport)
- Your **degree or diploma certificate**, and your transcript or results slip if you have not yet graduated
- Your **KCSE certificate**
- Your **KRA PIN** certificate (see our guide to [getting a KRA PIN online](/blog/how-to-get-a-kra-pin-online-in-kenya/))
- An up-to-date **CV** and a short cover letter
- A professional body registration or practising documents, if your field requires them

Keep file sizes small so they upload on a weak mobile connection, and name each file clearly, for example `Amina_Wanjiru_ID.pdf`.

## How to apply: step by step

1. **Find real adverts.** For government programmes, use the PSC's official recruitment portals and read the full advert. For private firms, go to the company's own career page. Never rely on a screenshot forwarded on WhatsApp.
2. **Check every eligibility line.** Look at the qualification level, the field, the year you must have graduated in or after, and whether people who have already done a public service internship are excluded.
3. **Create your account** on the portal with your correct ID number, a personal email you check daily and a phone number that works. Use the name exactly as it appears on your ID.
4. **Fill in the forms honestly.** Your education details must match your certificates. Mistakes here are a common reason applications are dropped.
5. **Upload your documents** and read the preview before you submit.
6. **Submit before the deadline**, not on the last day. Portals can slow down when thousands apply at the same time.
7. **Save proof.** Download or screenshot the confirmation, and keep the advert for reference.
8. **Watch your email and SMS** for shortlisting, and for posting letters if you are selected.

For private firms, the steps are similar, but you will usually send a tailored CV and cover letter and may face an interview.

## Mistakes that cost applicants

**Applying for everything.** Choose the roles that match your qualification and field.

**Sending the same CV everywhere.** Mirror the keywords in the advert, and show specific results, such as a project, a thesis topic or a society you led.

**Ignoring the cut-off year.** If an advert asks for graduates from a given year onwards and you graduated earlier, you will be screened out.

**Paying anyone.** Genuine government and company internships do not charge application fees. If someone asks for money to "secure a slot", report it and walk away.

**Forgetting your online presence.** Employers sometimes search for candidates, so keep your public social media professional.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is an internship the same as a job?** No. It is time-limited, aimed at building experience, and often comes with a stipend instead of a full salary. It may not lead to permanent employment, but it makes you a stronger candidate.

**Can I intern while I wait for my results?** Some private firms accept final-year students for attachments. Government programmes usually require a completed qualification, so read the advert.

**Do I need to be a graduate?** Not always. Certificates and diplomas, including TVET qualifications, are covered by some programmes and by the bill described above, depending on the advert.

**What stipend should I expect?** It differs between programmes and employers, and is often not stated in the advert. Ask the employer in writing before you accept.

**Will I get a certificate?** Programmes usually issue a certificate or letter at the end. Ask for a recommendation letter from your supervisor too.
