Picture a couple who, while dating, made time for each other every week: weekend trips, long phone calls, surprise visits after work. They expect marriage to make it easier. They will share a home, so there will be more time together, not less.

Six months after the wedding, it often feels the opposite. One partner gets home after a two-hour matatu ride and answers work emails at the dinner table. The other spends the evening on laundry, the shopping list and school fees. By 10pm both are exhausted. Affection becomes rare, and each partner quietly wonders what went wrong.

This pattern is common, and it is usually not a sign that love has faded. Very often, the real problem is **work**, and the way work spills into home life.

## What research says about work and relationships

Researchers call it **work-family spillover**: stress, tiredness and conflict from your job follow you home and affect your relationship. A briefing by the Tavistock Centre for Couple Relationships in the UK pulled together studies showing that:

- Conflict between work and family life is linked to lower relationship satisfaction, stress and burnout
- Long working hours are associated with more arguments and less interest in intimacy, mainly because people are simply too tired
- Checking work messages during family time is linked to higher stress at home

The effect works in a loop. Stress at work harms the relationship, and problems at home then make it harder to concentrate and perform at work.

A 2017 study published in *Community, Work & Family* followed 590 IT workers and their partners. Women whose partners regularly worked 50 or more hours a week reported more stress and lower relationship quality. The researchers found that it was the **added stress**, more than the lost time together, that did the damage.

## The "mental load"

Work is not only what happens in the office. Running a home involves constant planning: what to cook, when rent is due, which child needs new shoes, when the house help is coming. This invisible planning is often called the **mental load**, and in many homes one partner carries most of it.

A partner who finishes a paid job and then spends the evening managing the household has, in effect, worked two shifts. It is hard to feel relaxed, playful or affectionate with a to-do list running in your head.

## Desire works differently for different people

Sex educators describe two broad styles of desire:

- **Spontaneous desire** appears on its own, seemingly out of nowhere.
- **Responsive desire** builds gradually, after a person feels relaxed, connected and close.

The idea was first set out by Dr Rosemary Basson, and it applies to people of every gender, although surveys suggest responsive desire is more common among women. Both styles are normal. Problems start when partners assume the other works the same way they do. One may see a late-night advance as a way to unwind; the other, still carrying the day's stress, may experience it as one more demand. Neither is wrong, but without a conversation, both can feel rejected.

## Practical steps for working couples

1. **Protect a "work-free" window.** Agree on a time each evening when phones go down and work messages wait. Even 30 minutes makes a difference.
2. **Share the mental load, not just the chores.** Don't just "help" when asked; take full ownership of some tasks, such as paying the bills or managing school matters, including remembering them.
3. **Reconnect before you relax.** A short check-in after work ("How was your day, really?") reduces the stress you bring home.
4. **Show affection during the day.** Small, no-pressure gestures such as a message, a hug or making tea build closeness long before bedtime.
5. **Talk openly about needs.** Accept that desire and energy change in long relationships, and discuss it calmly instead of keeping score.
6. **Look at the job itself.** If one of you is regularly working very long hours, commuting for hours, or running a side hustle every evening, it may be the work, not the marriage, that needs to change.
7. **Get support if you need it.** Many employers' medical covers and employee wellness programmes include free or low-cost counselling, and couples counsellors can help partners talk through difficult issues.

## When the job is the problem

If your job leaves you drained every single day, it is worth asking whether it is sustainable. A role closer to home, a remote job that cuts out the commute, or more predictable hours can make a real difference to your family life.
